How it works

    

      A DNS query is a question with a type. Ask for A and you get IPv4 addresses;
      ask for MX and you get mail servers; ask for TXT and you get
      whatever the zone owner decided to put in a text record, which is increasingly where
      SPF, DKIM and domain-verification tokens live.
    

    

      Most sites hide the resolver behind an API. This one calls two public DNS-over-HTTPS
      endpoints directly from your browser and shows both answers side by side, because the
      interesting failures in DNS are the ones where the two disagree:
    

    
    
      
  • Stale cache. One resolver still has the old TTL; the other has
        already expired it. This is the most common cause of "it works on my machine".
    • 
      
  • Propagation. A recent change has reached one resolver and not the
        other. Compare the TTLs to estimate how long that will last.
    • 
      
  • Split horizon. The same name legitimately answers differently
        depending on where you ask from. If you see this, it is on purpose.
    • 
    

    

      Every answer on this page is a real query made when you pressed the button. Nothing is
      cached in the page and nothing is mocked.
    

  


  

    
What this cannot tell you

    
    
      
  • What your machine sees. Your laptop may use your ISP's resolver, a
        corporate DNS server, or a hosts file entry. This page shows what Cloudflare and
        Google see, which is useful for diagnosis but is not the same question.
    • 
      
  • DNSSEC validity. The AD flag is shown, but this is not
        a validator. Do not use it to sign off on a DNSSEC deployment.
    • 
      
  • UDP, TCP fallback or EDNS. DNS-over-HTTPS hides the transport, so
        you cannot see truncation or which transport actually carried the answer.
    • 
      
  • Private zones. Internal names will simply fail to resolve, which is
        expected and not a fault in the tool.
